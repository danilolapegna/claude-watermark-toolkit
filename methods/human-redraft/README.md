# Route 1: fresh human redraft

[Italiano](README.it.md) · [Compare every route](../../METHODS.md) · [Open Rewrite Room](https://danilolapegna.github.io/claude-watermark-toolkit/)

> **Best for:** short or important text, maximum privacy, no installation<br>
> **Time:** roughly 10 to 60 minutes<br>
> **Cost:** none, except your attention<br>
> **Text sent elsewhere:** no

## “So the brilliant method is: write it again?”

Yes. Duh.

This guide contains every route, including the obvious one. For a short text, a real human reconstruction can be stronger than an elaborate tool because it replaces the old wording while keeping the person who owns the ideas in control.

The useful trick is not typing twice. It is **closing the source before you draft**. If you keep looking at it, its sentence order and paragraph logic quietly follow you onto the new page.

## What you will have at the end

- a one-page fact sheet;
- a genuinely new draft in your own rhythm;
- a final fact check against the source;
- no provider account, upload or detector score.

## Before you start

You need the source, a blank page and a text whose ideas and final responsibility are yours. For direct quotations, legal language or fixed technical terms, mark the exact wording before you close the source.

## Step 1: make the fact sheet

Read the source once. Then fill this in with notes, not copied sentences:

```text
PURPOSE
What should the reader understand or do?

CLAIMS
- One idea or claim per line

EVIDENCE AND QUALIFICATIONS
- Numbers, examples, uncertainty, exceptions

KEEP EXACT
- Names, dates, URLs, citations, direct quotations

VOICE
- Three things I naturally do when I write
- Three words or habits I would never use
```

If a line on the fact sheet reads like a polished sentence, shorten it. The sheet should preserve meaning, not prose.

## Step 2: close the source

Actually close it. Do not leave it in a tab beside the blank page. Keep only the fact sheet visible.

Choose the order that helps this reader now. It does not need to match the source unless the logic truly depends on that sequence.

## Step 3: write the new draft

Write from the fact sheet in your normal rhythm. Do not stop after every sentence to compare. If a transition feels awkward, solve it from the argument in front of you rather than reopening the source.

## Step 4: reopen for facts, not phrases

Now compare source and draft. Check each item in this order:

1. Every claim is present and still means the same thing.
2. Numbers, names, dates, links and direct quotations are exact.
3. Degrees of certainty have not become stronger or weaker.
4. No new example or conclusion slipped in.
5. The draft still serves the same reader and purpose.

Only after those checks should you look at phrase overlap. A factually broken text with fresh wording is still broken.

## Step 5: make it sound like you

Read the result aloud. Mark every sentence you would not naturally say. Rewrite those sentences once, without looking at the source.

## Common failures and the recovery

| What happened | Why | What to do now |
|---|---|---|
| The draft still follows the same paragraphs | The source stayed visible | Close it and reorder the claim notes before another pass |
| A number or qualification disappeared | The fact sheet was too loose | Add it to KEEP EXACT or EVIDENCE, then repair the draft |
| The text sounds polished but generic | You edited toward “professional” instead of your voice | Restore concrete voice habits and read aloud again |
| The source is too long to hold in one sheet | This route has reached its useful limit | Use the [two-envelope clean room](../two-envelope-clean-room/README.md) section by section |

## What success looks like

The facts survive, the argument still belongs to you and the draft no longer depends on the source sentences. That is a meaningful result. It is not a certificate against Anthropic's private detector.
